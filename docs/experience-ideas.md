# Twenty possibilities for the GR book

Proposals, not implemented features. The strongest first investments are symbol inspection, unfolding derivations, and voice control of vetted visualizations. Each interaction should stay out of the way until it helps the reader, preserve accessible text and reduced-motion alternatives, and distinguish physical measurements from coordinate choices.

1. **Inspect any symbol.** Tap a symbol for its meaning, mathematical type, units, and role in that particular equation, with a direct connection to the relevant figure.
2. **Unfold a derivation.** Reveal one justified step at a time, with matching terms moving between equations and a plain-language explanation of what changed.
3. **Conduct experiments by voice.** Ask the tutor to rotate a scene, adjust its mass, compare two observers, or point to a particular term using the scene's supported controls.
4. **Connect equations to measurements.** Drag an object in a visualization and watch its measurements, graph, and corresponding equation update together.
5. **Change coordinates without changing the physics.** Show two descriptions of the same experiment side by side, keeping invariant readings visibly linked.
6. **Draw a worldline.** Sketch two allowed journeys between shared events and compare the proper time accumulated along each route.
7. **Ride Einstein's elevator.** Move between supported, accelerating, and freely falling laboratories and investigate clocks, light pulses, and accelerometer readings.
8. **Look through a curvature microscope.** Transport an arrow around shrinking loops and relate the residual rotation to the loop's area and orientation.
9. **Run a lensing observatory.** Move a light source behind a mass and connect the images you see to their corresponding light paths.
10. **Cross a black-hole horizon.** Follow a falling observer using synchronized views of their clock, light signals, coordinates, and measured tidal forces.
11. **Build a working GPS system.** Extend the existing clock lab into a constellation and receiver, then observe timing and position errors when individual corrections are omitted.
12. **Predict, then reveal.** Occasionally ask for a physical prediction before an experiment runs, with an explanation that responds to the chosen answer.
13. **Test a misconception.** Turn statements such as “curved coordinate lines prove curvature” into concise counterexample experiments.
14. **Work in a mathematical scratchpad.** Try algebra beside the book with checks for dimensions, index consistency, and the first invalid step.
15. **Bring prerequisites into the margin.** Open a short calculus or linear-algebra refresher at the exact point of difficulty, then return to the same sentence.
16. **Explain it back.** Describe an idea aloud and receive focused feedback on the conceptual gap rather than a generic score.
17. **Give narration a visual score.** Author timed scene cues so the camera, relevant objects, and equation terms guide attention as each explanation is spoken.
18. **Keep a personal field notebook.** Save an equation, scene state, question, or spoken insight together with its exact place in the course.
19. **Explore a map of understanding.** Trace prerequisites visually and find the shortest path from familiar mathematics to a chosen idea such as black-hole horizons.
20. **Let one experiment grow through the book.** Revisit the same clocks, falling laboratories, and light pulses as successive chapters reveal deeper mathematical descriptions.
